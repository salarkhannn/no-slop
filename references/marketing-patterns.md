# Marketing and business page patterns

Use for landing pages, company pages, product marketing, feature pages, pricing, comparison,
case studies, and conversion-oriented business sites.

## 1. Narrative strategy

Build a page around one audience, one valuable outcome, and one principal conversion.

A useful sequence:

1. clear promise;
2. immediate evidence;
3. explanation of mechanism;
4. concrete use cases or capabilities;
5. trust and risk reduction;
6. plan, next step, or conversion.

Not every page needs every section. Remove sections that repeat a claim without adding
evidence.

This sequence is a reasoning aid, not a template. Do not automatically translate it into
a centered hero, logo strip, three feature cards, testimonials, pricing, FAQ, and final
CTA. Let the available proof and the buyer's objections determine the actual composition.

Content requirements:

- write a specific headline, not a category slogan;
- make subcopy explain audience, mechanism, or constraint;
- use real feature names, outcomes, and data shapes;
- keep proof near the claim it supports;
- use one primary call to action and one legitimate alternative at most.

Familiar patterns are allowed when the content supports them. A centered hero may be the
best composition for one focused promise; equal cards may be right for true peers. Judge
the accumulation and evidence, not the familiarity of one pattern.

## 2. Header and hero

Header:

- keep navigation labels concrete;
- reduce top-level choices;
- visually distinguish primary conversion without turning every link into a button;
- provide a usable narrow-screen menu with focus management;
- keep sticky behavior compact and non-obstructive.

Hero:

- establish promise and audience within the first scan;
- keep text width controlled;
- place evidence, product visual, or direct demonstration near the claim;
- avoid decorative imagery that competes with the action;
- use display type only when the content earns it;
- preserve useful content above the fold without optimizing to one screen height.

Hero composition options:

- split: copy plus product image or proof;
- centered: one focused promise with compact evidence;
- editorial: offset columns and narrative hierarchy;
- interactive: small live product demonstration with safe fallback.

Do not default to a centered hero merely because the page is marketing.

## 3. Evidence and feature sections

Evidence can be:

- product screenshot with annotation;
- measured outcome with source context;
- concise customer quotation;
- recognizable customer or partner;
- workflow before/after;
- case study;
- security, reliability, or compliance proof;
- transparent product detail.

Internal audit records, self-awarded quality scores, mock “ready” states, invented usage,
and fabricated operational data are not evidence and must never appear as proof.

Feature composition:

- group capabilities around user outcomes;
- give important capabilities more space;
- alternate composition only when it improves narrative rhythm;
- use icon grids for genuinely peer features, not as a default section generator;
- pair abstract claims with concrete UI or example;
- keep each section's visual grammar related to the whole page.

Section rhythm:

- alternate information density, not random background colors;
- allow major sections generous separation;
- use a recurring container and alignment system;
- create a few deliberate breakouts rather than letting every section break the grid.

## 4. Pricing and comparison

Pricing:

- identify intended customer for each plan;
- state billing period, currency, and meaningful limits;
- distinguish included, capped, and add-on features;
- make the recommended plan defensible;
- explain trial, cancellation, and contact requirements;
- keep primary plan actions comparable.

Comparison:

- organize by customer decision, not internal feature catalog;
- keep headers visible for long tables;
- use text with icons for important availability states;
- provide a narrow-screen strategy;
- disclose exceptions near the relevant claim.

Do not exaggerate one plan using disproportionate visual weight if it obscures comparison.

## 5. Calls to action

CTA hierarchy:

- one primary conversion style across the page;
- secondary action remains visibly secondary;
- repeat the primary action after new evidence or at natural decision points;
- adapt copy to the user's stage: “View demo,” “Start trial,” and “Contact sales” are
  different commitments;
- explain friction or prerequisites near the action.
- choose and record the component and variant, not only button copy and color;
- for the rare principal hero and final conversion actions, compare Fancy Button against
  Button or Link Button and record why its expressiveness is earned or rejected.

Final CTA:

- restate outcome, not the whole hero;
- resolve the largest remaining objection;
- avoid an oversized gradient panel that contains only a repeated button.
- do not default to a full-width solid-color band as the anti-gradient replacement;
- change topology, proof, or interaction when the final section needs renewed interest.

## 6. Responsive composition

At narrow widths:

- prioritize promise, proof, and action;
- stack split layouts in reading order;
- crop product visuals around the meaningful region;
- keep tap targets generous;
- avoid horizontal logo or card carousels that hide evidence;
- transform comparison tables deliberately;
- reduce display type enough to prevent awkward one-word lines;
- keep section rhythm without copying desktop vertical space exactly.

At wide widths:

- cap reading width;
- do not stretch body copy across the viewport;
- use grid span and negative space to create hierarchy;
- keep product screenshots large enough to be understood.

## 7. Motion

Appropriate:

- a subtle product demonstration;
- section reveal that preserves reading order;
- diagram or workflow explanation;
- state change inside an interactive example;
- restrained milestone or confirmation.

Usually inappropriate:

- every section animating on scroll;
- continuous floating decoration;
- parallax on essential text;
- staggered card grids repeated throughout the page;
- motion that delays access to proof or action.

Motion must stop or simplify for reduced-motion preferences and must not create layout shift.

## 8. Anti-patterns

- vague category headline;
- generic startup gradient;
- centered copy in every section;
- three equal cards repeated several times;
- logo wall without context;
- invented metrics or placeholder testimonials;
- feature names without examples;
- excessive pills and rounded containers;
- decorative dashboard mockup too small to read;
- multiple competing conversion buttons;
- pricing differences hidden in fine print;
- scroll animation as the primary source of interest.
- giant headline + tiny mono eyebrow + sparse ruled sections as the primary source of
  anti-slop identity;
- fake scorecards or decision records used as product proof;
- the complete hero → three cards → testimonials → pricing → CTA sequence without
  evidence that each section advances the decision;
- generic icon tiles or bento grids used to manufacture feature count;
- vague technology copy paired with gradients, glass, glows, or decorative dashboard
  imagery.

## 9. Completion checklist

- [ ] Audience, promise, and conversion are explicit
- [ ] Proof sits near major claims
- [ ] Hero composition matches the content
- [ ] Section rhythm is deliberate
- [ ] Feature hierarchy is not a uniform grid by default
- [ ] Pricing is transparent and comparable
- [ ] CTA language matches commitment
- [ ] Product visuals remain legible and truthful
- [ ] Responsive order preserves narrative
- [ ] Motion explains or demonstrates rather than decorates
